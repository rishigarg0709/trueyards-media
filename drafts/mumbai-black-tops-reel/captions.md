## Claim ledger
- Claim: the black on old Mumbai facades is mostly living growth (algae, cyanobacteria, fungi) on wet plaster, not soot.
- Proof: Mumbai Santacruz averages about 2,502 mm of rain a year, about 95% in June to September (IMD normals 1991 to 2020); facades that stay wet grow a dark biofilm (RIBuild); dark stains follow run-off under parapets and sills (LNEC facade study).
- Caveat: soot and dirt can add to it; a repaint without cleaning and waterproofing rarely lasts. Education only.

If your building in Mumbai has turned black under the terrace, this is for you.
That black layer is mostly not pollution. It is algae, cyanobacteria and fungus growing on plaster that stays wet for months.
Mumbai gets about two and a half metres of rain a year, almost all of it between June and September, and the water runs off the parapet and window ledges straight down the wall. That is exactly where the darkest lines appear.
So painting over it rarely lasts. The growth has to be cleaned, cracks sealed and the parapet waterproofed first.

## LinkedIn
Title: Why old Mumbai buildings turn black at the top
For a society or a buyer looking at an older Mumbai building, the black facade is a maintenance signal, not just a cosmetic one.
The dark layer is mostly biological growth (algae, cyanobacteria, fungi) on plaster that stays wet through about 2.5 metres of monsoon rain a year, concentrated where water runs off parapets and sills.
Paint makers' own guidance puts cleaning the growth, sealing cracks and waterproofing the parapet before any anti-algal coat, so the repair budget belongs in the waterproofing line, not just the paint line.
Sources: IMD Santacruz normals 1991 to 2020; RIBuild; LNEC facade study; Berger Paints.
